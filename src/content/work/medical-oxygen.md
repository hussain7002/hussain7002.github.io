COVID-19's second wave in 2021 turned medical oxygen from a background utility into a national emergency. In the months that followed, an enormous amount of oxygen infrastructure was installed across India very quickly: oxygen plants on hospital premises, liquid oxygen tanks, pipelines to the wards and large numbers of oxygen concentrators.

By the time I joined this work at PATH in 2022, the pandemic had changed shape. The emergency was over, and the headlines had moved on. But states were left with a great deal to deal with, and many decisions to make. A plant installed in a hurry still has to be run every day, maintained every month and repaired when it fails. Someone has to decide which equipment to keep using, how to train the people who operate it, and how much oxygen each hospital actually needs. In some ways, this was the harder part of the story, because there was no longer a crisis to make it urgent.

## The work

Under USAID- and Gates Foundation-funded initiatives, I worked as technical adviser on medical oxygen to the governments of Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu and Telangana, covering about a hundred district hospitals.

The work covered the whole oxygen system, from where oxygen is made or stored to the patient's bedside: pressure swing adsorption (PSA) plants, which make oxygen on site from the air; liquid oxygen tanks and the supply chain that fills them; medical gas pipelines; oxygen concentrators; the way oxygen is administered to patients; and fire safety, because oxygen makes everything burn faster.

Much of it began with seeing things for myself. I carried out facility assessments to understand what each hospital had, what condition it was in and what it needed. Those visits taught me more than any document could, because most of the problems I found were not in the equipment specifications. They were in the conditions around the equipment.

## A lesson in concrete

One example has stayed with me.

A PSA plant works by drawing in large volumes of air, compressing it and passing it through a material called a molecular sieve, which separates the oxygen from the nitrogen. Everything about that process is sensitive to what comes in with the air. Dust is one of a plant's worst enemies. It clogs intake filters, wears out compressors and can damage the sieve itself, shortening the life of an expensive plant and reducing the purity of the oxygen it produces.

That is why PSA plants are meant to sit in a shed built to a particular standard: clean, ventilated and with a properly finished floor. In the rush to install plants during the pandemic, that standard was not always met. In some sheds, the concrete floor had not been cured properly. Concrete that is not given time to cure develops a weak surface that keeps shedding fine dust as it is walked on and as equipment vibrates. Sweeping does not solve it. However often the floor is cleaned, it keeps producing dust, and it remains a permanent problem unless the surface is treated or sealed.

It was a small detail with a large consequence: a plant worth a great deal of money being slowly damaged by the floor it stood on. And it was a lesson I have carried into all my work since. The success of a piece of technology often depends on the ordinary things around it, which nobody thinks about when it is being bought.

## Tools for planning

Alongside the advice, we built practical tools. One was a **demand estimation tool**, which worked out a facility's oxygen needs from its bed capacity and other parameters, so that states could plan supply and decide which sources of oxygen made sense for which hospitals.

For anyone working on medical oxygen, the [resources section of the Global Oxygen Alliance](https://globaloxygenalliance.org/resources/) is a good place to start. It brings together national oxygen plans, technical guides and primers from many countries and organisations, and you may well find something useful there.

## Guidance for the region

The work also extended beyond India. I contributed to technical guidance and training on oxygen and respiratory care equipment for the WHO South-East Asia Regional Office, working with WHO country teams across the region, including Indonesia and Timor-Leste. In 2026, WHO SEARO published two products from this work:

- the [*Regional manual with operating guides on respiratory care equipment and devices*](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789292800055), which WHO describes as aiming to "provide foundational information on key biomedical aspects of maintaining respiratory care equipment", written for biomedical engineers, technicians and related staff; and
- the [*Generic training modules on oxygen/respiratory care equipment*](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789292800062).

Seeing them published was satisfying, because they put into a lasting, regional form much of what we had learned in hospitals in three Indian states.

## Getting knowledge to the people who need it

One problem kept coming back. The knowledge needed to keep oxygen systems running existed, in manuals, guidance documents and training material. But much of it was in English, and it was not always in the hands of the technician facing a problem in a district hospital at night. That gap led us to build a multilingual WhatsApp chatbot for oxygen equipment, which I have written about [separately](/writing/oxychat-small-ai/).

## What I took from it

Emergencies get attention; maintenance does not. Yet most of the value of the investment made during the pandemic depends on what happens afterwards: whether plants are maintained, whether technicians are trained and supported, and whether the conditions around the equipment are right. That work is slow and unglamorous, and it rarely makes the news. It is also what decides whether a hospital has oxygen the next time it is needed.
