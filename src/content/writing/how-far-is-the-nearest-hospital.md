**Key points**
- Measured in a straight line, 98% of India's people live within 5 km of some public health facility, mostly a sub-centre.
- The picture changes at the next level of care. Only 36% live within 10 km of a public hospital, and about 477 million people, a third of the country, live more than 20 km from one.
- Most of those people are not in the desert or the mountains. They live in the densely populated plains, in the gaps between hospitals.

Ask how far Indians live from health care and the answer depends on which care you mean. For the first point of contact, the answer is reassuring: a public facility is almost always close by. For a hospital, the place you go for a caesarean section, a blood transfusion or a broken leg, distance is still a real barrier for hundreds of millions of people. And when you weight the map by where people actually live, the places that matter most are not the empty corners of the country but its crowded heartland.

## How I measured it

I took a public list of 227,091 government health facilities with coordinates, from the National Identification Number (NIN) facility directory (April 2026), and the Survey of India's state boundaries. I divided India into about 4.1 million squares of roughly 1 km each and, for every square, found the straight-line distance to the nearest facility. I did this three times: for any facility, for primary facilities (sub-centres, primary and urban health centres, dispensaries) and for public hospitals (district, sub-district or taluk, and medical college hospitals; 2,216 in the list).

Then I weighted each square by the number of people living in it, using WorldPop's 2020 population estimates, which spread census and survey counts across a 1 km grid. That gives two views of the same map: one of land, one of people.

Two choices are worth stating plainly. First, I grouped all public hospitals together rather than separating district hospitals from medical colleges, because states label their hospitals differently in the list: Haryana's civil hospitals, for example, appear as sub-district or taluk hospitals, so "district hospital" alone would make Haryana look emptier than it is. Second, straight-line distance always understates the real journey. Roads bend, rivers need bridges, and hills add hours. A companion piece follows the roads in Telangana; this one asks the simpler question first.

## First contact is close; the hospital is not

| Distance (straight line) | Any public facility | A public hospital |
|---|---|---|
| Within 5 km | 98% of people | 19% |
| Within 10 km | 99.8% | 36% |
| Within 20 km | 99.96% | 65% |
| More than 20 km | about 0.5 million people | about 477 million people |
| More than 50 km | about 40,000 people | about 28 million people |

The first column is a quiet achievement. India built a dense layer of sub-centres and primary health centres over decades, and the map shows it: outside a few deserts, mountains and islands, a public facility is within walking or short-bus distance for nearly everyone. Primary facilities alone give almost the same result as all facilities, because sub-centres make up most of the list.

The second column is where the pressure is. Emergency obstetric care, surgery, blood and trauma care sit mostly in hospitals. Half of India's people live more than about 15 km from one in a straight line, which in practice often means an hour or more by road.

## Land and people tell different stories

Averaged over land, the distance to a public hospital is about 30 km. Averaged over people, it is 17 km. The gap exists because the largest empty spaces, the Thar desert, the high Himalaya and eastern Ladakh, are where few people live.

So the first map, shaded by distance, draws the eye to the wrong places. The dark patches in western Rajasthan, Kachchh and Ladakh are real, and the people who live there do face extraordinary distances. But they are few. The second map shows only the people who live more than 20 km from a public hospital, and the colour concentrates somewhere else entirely: across the plains of Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, through central India, along the eastern coast. These are not remote places. They are crowded places where the hospitals are spaced far apart relative to the number of people between them.

The state averages make the same point from another angle. Delhi and Chandigarh sit at about 3 km, Kerala at 7 and Tamil Nadu at 10, reflecting dense hospital networks. Most large states sit between about 15 and 22 km, with Rajasthan (25) and Odisha (28) the farthest of them. Ladakh (48 km), Arunachal Pradesh (33) and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands (30) are the farthest of all.

Read the island rows with care. The facility list has only 55 entries for the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, fewer than the islands actually have, so some of that distance reflects gaps in the list rather than gaps in care. The desert and mountain rows are more robust: these are genuinely sparse places where a hospital trip is a journey of half a day or more.

## What the maps suggest

Our interpretation, not a verdict. The first layer of the health system, the one that brings immunisation, antenatal check-ups and basic treatment to the village, is close to universal in physical terms. The frontier has moved up a level, to the referral system: how quickly someone can get from that first contact to a hospital that can operate, transfuse or resuscitate.

That frontier looks different in two kinds of places. In the deserts, mountains and islands, building more hospitals will rarely close a 150 km gap; what matters there is transport (road, boat or air), birth waiting homes, and making the nearest facility as capable as possible. In the crowded plains, the numbers are so large that small improvements matter: a community health centre upgraded to handle emergency obstetric care, or an ambulance network that reliably covers the gaps between hospitals, can bring care within reach of millions.

The analysis also shows how much depends on the facility list itself. Inconsistent type labels and missing entries change the answer, which is one reason I care about clean, standard facility registries.

India is not alone in measuring this. The Lancet Commission on Global Surgery proposed that 80% of people in every country should be able to reach a hospital that can perform a caesarean section, laparotomy and treatment of open fractures within two hours. Global travel-time maps by the Malaria Atlas Project make the same kind of calculation along roads for the whole world.

## Limits

- **Straight line, not travel time.** Real journeys are longer and slower, especially in hills, forests and during the monsoon.
- **Public facilities only.** Private hospitals, which deliver much of India's hospital care, are not in the list.
- **Location is not capability.** A hospital on the map may lack a surgeon, an anaesthetist or blood on a given day.
- **The facility list is uneven.** Labels differ between states and some areas, notably the islands, have fewer entries than they should.
- **Population is modelled.** WorldPop estimates where people live from census and other data; it is very good at this scale but not a head count, and it does not cover the northernmost areas within the Survey of India boundary.
