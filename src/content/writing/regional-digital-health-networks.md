Digital health has no shortage of networks. There are global initiatives, regional collaboratives, communities of practice, coalitions, alliances and working groups. Many are launched with energy and a declaration. Fewer are still useful three years later.

The South-East Asia Region now has its own: **SEA-DAC**, the South-East Asia Digital Health and AI Collaborative Network, set up through the WHO South-East Asia Regional Committee. It sits within a larger global architecture. So it is a good moment to ask a practical question: **what can a regional network actually do that countries cannot do alone, and what does it take to do it well?**

## The global layer: GIDH

In 2023, under India's G20 presidency, health ministers backed a **Global Initiative on Digital Health (GIDH)**, which WHO launched in early 2024 as a WHO-managed "network of networks". It was designed to fix a familiar problem: countries' digital health needs and the money and tools on offer rarely meet. Donors fund what they want to fund, countries end up with dozens of disconnected pilots, and nobody has the full picture.

GIDH organises its work around four ideas:
1. **A country needs tracker**, so that investment follows what countries say they need.
2. **A country resource portal**, mapping who is funding what.
3. **A transformation toolbox** of quality-assured tools and guidance.
4. **Convening and knowledge exchange** across networks.

These are good ideas, and they are global. But much of what makes digital health work or fail is regional and national: languages, health system structures, regulatory traditions, and which countries have already solved a problem their neighbours are about to face. That is where a regional network earns its place.

## What a regional network can do that countries cannot

**1. Let countries learn from neighbours, not just from donors.** India has built national health IDs and registries at enormous scale. Thailand has decades of experience with universal coverage and health data. Sri Lanka, Bhutan, Nepal, Bangladesh, Indonesia, Maldives and Timor-Leste each have experience of their own. A health official in one country often learns more from a peer two borders away, whose system looks like theirs, than from a global guideline. Peer exchange is the network's most valuable product, and the cheapest.

**2. Match needs to expertise.** When a ministry needs help, for example writing a national AI policy, setting up a health data standards body, or evaluating an AI tool, a regional network that knows who has done it before can connect them within weeks. That matchmaking role is GIDH's needs tracker, made local.

**3. Speak with one voice where it helps.** On issues such as AI regulation, cross-border data and vendor contracts, small countries have little leverage alone. Common regional positions and shared evaluation methods give each of them more.

**4. Turn global guidance into regional practice.** WHO's Global Strategy on Digital Health has been extended to 2027, and a new strategy for 2028 to 2033 is being shaped through regional consultations. A regional network is the natural route for countries' experience to shape that strategy, and for its guidance to be adapted to the region's realities.

**5. Build a regional workforce.** Every country in the region is short of people who understand both health and technology. Shared training, fellowships and communities of practice spread scarce expertise further.

## What makes a network useful rather than ceremonial

From watching many networks, and being part of some, a few things separate the useful from the ceremonial:

- **A small, permanent secretariat that does the work between meetings.** Networks run on follow-up. Someone has to track what each country asked for, who promised what, and what happened next.
- **Country focal points with real authority.** If the person representing a ministry cannot speak for it, the network becomes a seminar.
- **A workplan tied to country requests, not partner agendas.** The best test of any activity is whether a member state asked for it.
- **Concrete products.** A shared evaluation method for AI tools, a template for a national digital health strategy, a list of who has expertise in what. Products outlast meetings.
- **Honest monitoring.** Count requests answered and practices adopted, not webinars held.
- **Young professionals in the room.** The people who will run these systems in 2040 are early in their careers now.

On that last point: I am a member of **YO.DH (Youth Ownership of Digital Health)**, a community launched by PATH in 2023 with support from the Gates Foundation and partners, which by 2025 had more than a thousand members under 35 across more than forty countries. I was featured in its September 2024 issue. What YO.DH shows is that young professionals across countries are hungry to learn from each other and will do so with very little structure, if someone makes the connection. Regional networks would benefit from building that energy in deliberately.

## The risk to avoid

The most common failure of networks is becoming a calendar of events. Meetings are easy to organise and to report. The harder, more valuable work is quieter: answering a ministry's specific question within a month, connecting two officials who should know each other, making sure a good practice from one country is actually tried in another.

If SEA-DAC can be measured by that kind of work, the questions it answered, the connections it made, the practices that crossed borders, it will become something countries rely on. That is the only real measure of a network.
