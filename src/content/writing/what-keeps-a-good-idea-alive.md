Every public health practitioner has seen a pilot succeed. The numbers improve, the district team is proud, the report is well received, and a presentation travels from one meeting to the next. And then, a year or two later, the work has quietly stopped. Nothing went wrong, exactly. The project ended, the staff moved on, and the routine of the system closed over the space where the innovation had been.

A successful pilot is a milestone, not proof. It shows that an idea can work under particular conditions: extra people, extra attention, extra money and a team that cares. What it does not show is whether the idea can survive once those conditions are gone. That is a different question, and it is answered by different things.

In my experience, a handful of things matter most, and most of them have little to do with the innovation itself.

The first is **ownership**: whether someone inside the system, an officer, a department or a programme, regards the work as theirs rather than as a partner's project they are hosting. The second is **money in the right place**: whether the cost has found its way into a government budget line, however small, rather than sitting entirely in a project budget. The third is **fit with routine work**: whether the innovation has become part of how a frontline worker or a district manager already does their job, or remains an extra task alongside it.

The fourth is **where the idea sits in relation to the rules**. An innovation that sits comfortably inside existing guidelines, cadres and procurement rules can be absorbed with a circular. One that sits at the edge of the rules, such as a new task for a cadre whose role is defined elsewhere, a payment no scheme provides for, or a device no standard yet covers, needs someone to change or interpret the rules before it can last. A pilot can run on a special permission; a programme cannot.

The fifth is **boundaries**: between departments, between levels of government, and between people. Many good ideas in public health need more than one department to work together, such as health and women and child development, health and labour, or a state and a private estate. Convergence sounds simple in a meeting and is hard in practice, because each department has its own budget, targets, reporting lines and timelines. An innovation that depends on two departments cooperating lasts only as long as that cooperation does, unless it is written into how both of them work.

And running through all of it is **bureaucracy**, in the neutral sense of the word: files, approvals, transfers and the annual planning cycle. An officer who championed a pilot may be transferred before it ends. A proposal that misses the window for the annual programme plan may wait a year. None of this is a failure of the system. It is how a large public system keeps itself accountable. But an innovation that is not designed to travel through those processes rarely survives them.

None of these show up in a pilot's results table. A pilot can score well on every indicator and still be weak on all of them.

In 2025, many people working in global health saw this play out at scale. In January, a United States executive order titled *Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid* paused foreign development assistance for review. In the months that followed, many colleagues across countries saw the programmes they worked on come to an end. Whatever one's view of the decision, the period worked as an unplanned test of sustainability. Across many countries, the work that carried on tended to be the work that governments had already taken in as their own: protocols adopted into state guidelines, tools that ran on government systems, and people trained who stayed in post.

I saw this from the inside. The project I was part of, USAID's Saksham, was one of only two USAID projects in India to continue. I would not claim to know every reason why. But I noticed how much of what had worked was by then sitting inside government processes rather than beside them, and how much difference that made to what could continue.

The lesson I take from it is not about any one funder. Funding of every kind, from donors, philanthropies or government schemes, changes over time. An innovation that depends on one source of support is fragile, however good its results. So the question worth asking at the start of a pilot is not only "will this work?" but also "**whose will this be when we leave?**"

A pilot that answers the first question earns the right to be taken seriously. Only the second question decides whether it lasts.
