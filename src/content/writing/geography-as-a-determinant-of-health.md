Public health talks a lot about the social determinants of health: income, education, gender, caste. Those matter enormously. But there is an older determinant that sits underneath them and is easy to forget because it does not change with policy: **where you are on the earth.** The rock under your village, the water in your aquifer, the forest at the edge of your fields, the river that floods every monsoon.

Geography does not care who is in government or how much a family earns. In the five places below, the pattern holds across countries and across centuries. And in each case, the fix was never a better clinic. It was changing what geography delivers to people's bodies.

## 1. Mountains and iodine: the goitre belts

During the ice ages, glaciers and heavy rainfall leached iodine out of mountain soils. Crops grown in those soils carry little iodine, and people who eat them do not get enough. The result, without intervention, is goitre and, far worse, iodine deficiency during pregnancy, which damages the developing brain of the child. Iodine deficiency is the world's most common preventable cause of intellectual disability.

The pattern is the same wherever there are high mountains: the **Himalayas**, the **Alps**, the **Andes**. Switzerland, with its Alpine goitre and cretinism, was one of the first countries to iodise salt, in 1922. India's goitre belt ran along the sub-Himalayan region from Jammu and Kashmir to the North-East. India began a National Goitre Control Programme in 1962, which became the National Iodine Deficiency Disorders Control Programme in 1992 when the goal widened to universal iodised salt.

**The fix:** not medicine, but salt. The geography stayed the same; what changed was the supply chain.

## 2. The delta and arsenic

The **Bengal delta**, shared by West Bengal and Bangladesh, is built from young river sediments carried down from the Himalayas. Under certain conditions, those sediments release naturally occurring arsenic into shallow groundwater.

In the 1970s and 1980s, millions of hand-pumped tube wells were installed across the region to give people safe water, free of the bacteria that caused deadly diarrhoea in surface water. It worked for diarrhoea. But nobody tested the wells for arsenic. Years later, skin lesions, cancers and other diseases revealed that tens of millions of people had been drinking arsenic-contaminated water. A 2000 paper in the Bulletin of the World Health Organization called it "the largest mass poisoning of a population in history".

The same geology produces arsenic problems in other river deltas and basins, including the **Mekong** and the **Red River** in Vietnam and Cambodia.

**The lesson:** a public health intervention that ignores geology can create a new epidemic while solving an old one.

## 3. Granite and fluoride: Nalgonda

Fluoride in small amounts protects teeth. In large amounts, over years, it damages them, then bones, causing skeletal fluorosis: stiff, bent, painful spines and limbs, in some cases crippling. High fluoride comes from the rocks that groundwater passes through, especially some granites.

**Nalgonda** in Telangana became the best-known fluorosis area in India; generations of families there grew up with mottled teeth and many developed bone deformities. India has fluorosis-endemic districts in many states. Across the world, the same problem affects the **East African Rift Valley**, including parts of Ethiopia and Kenya, and parts of **China**.

**The fix:** change the water source. Defluoridation at household level has been tried for decades with limited success, because it depends on daily effort. What has worked better is bringing in piped surface water from rivers and reservoirs, which Telangana has done at scale.

## 4. Forests and malaria

Malaria in India is concentrated where forests and hills meet poor roads. **Odisha**, with a small share of India's population, has for years accounted for a large share of its malaria cases, concentrated in forested, tribal and hard-to-reach districts. The mosquito species that thrive in forest streams and the movement of people into forests for work keep transmission going, and the same terrain that shelters mosquitoes keeps clinics far away.

The pattern repeats across the **Greater Mekong**: in Cambodia, Myanmar, Laos and Thailand, malaria has been driven by "forest-goers", people who work or sleep in forests, long after transmission fell in villages.

Odisha's response, the DAMaN programme ("malaria elimination in inaccessible areas" in Odia), took testing, treatment and bed nets to remote villages through mass screening camps, and was followed by steep declines in cases.

**The lesson:** where geography keeps people far from care, care has to travel.

## 5. Rivers and floods: the Brahmaputra

Around 40% of Assam's land area is officially classed as flood-prone. Every monsoon, the **Brahmaputra** and its tributaries flood villages, cut roads, and isolate communities for weeks. Floods bring diarrhoeal disease and snakebite, destroy crops and food stores, and, critically for my own field, cut pregnant women off from the facilities where they could give birth safely.

The same story plays out downstream in Bangladesh's river islands (chars), and in other monsoon floodplains across South and South-East Asia.

**The lesson:** flood calendars should be part of health plans. Birth waiting homes, boats as ambulances, and pre-positioned medicines in places that will be cut off are as much maternal health interventions as any clinical protocol.

## What ties these together

1. **Geography acts through a pathway.** Soil to food (iodine), rock to water (arsenic, fluoride), landscape to vectors (malaria), river to access (floods). Find the pathway and you find the intervention.
2. **The best fixes are rarely clinical.** Salt, water sources, mass screening in place, transport plans. They sit across ministries: food, water, rural development, disaster management.
3. **Health data must be mapped.** None of these patterns is visible in a state average. They appear only when data is plotted on a map at district, block and village level. That is why GIS is not a luxury for health planning. In urban health, mapping vulnerability ward by ward, slum by slum, does the same job for cities.

Geography is the determinant we cannot change. What we can change is how well our health systems see it.
