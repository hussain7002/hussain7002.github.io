There is something unusual about digital as a field: **one person can pick it up.** A capable individual, or a small team, can take a tool that is already proven elsewhere and deploy it. Much of what a health system needs, from data collection apps to health information systems, now exists as open-source software and digital public goods. These are tools whose code, standards or content are openly available for anyone to use, adapt and build on. A district or a country does not have to start from nothing. It can start from what others have already built.

That changes where the real difficulty lies. The bottleneck is rarely the software. It is **knowledge**: knowing which tools exist, which ones worked in a setting like yours, what went wrong when someone else tried, and who you could call to ask. That knowledge is scattered. Some of it sits inside ministries and state departments, some inside development partners and implementing organisations, and a great deal of it sits inside individual people who happened to do the work.

This is why I have come to think of networks as essential infrastructure for digital health, not an optional extra.

## Too big for any one organisation

Digital health touches every part of a health system: how a frontline worker records a visit, how a facility orders medicines, how a district reviews its performance, how a ministry plans its budget. No single organisation, however large, covers all of it. Development partners and implementing organisations do significant work, often in several countries at once. But the knowledge each of them builds tends to stay inside the organisation: in project reports, internal drives and the heads of people who eventually move on.

That is a loss for everyone. A state in one country may be about to solve a problem that a neighbouring country solved three years ago. A government team may be evaluating a tool that another government has already tested. If the knowledge does not travel, each of them pays for the same lesson twice.

Networks are how it travels. At their best, they gather what organisations and individuals have learned and get it to the people who can act on it, especially government stakeholders, who can then take open tools and deploy them in their own systems at little or no licence cost. At the global level, WHO describes its Global Initiative on Digital Health as "a WHO Managed Network of organizations, institutions and government technical agencies" supporting national digital health transformation. But much of the most useful exchange happens on a smaller scale: between practitioners in neighbouring states, between programme teams facing the same problem, between people early in their careers who are building the same kinds of systems in different countries.

## Trying to bring it together in one place

I saw the value of this most clearly under USAID's Saksham project. Over its life, the project and its partners generated a large body of practical knowledge about maternal, newborn and child health: what had been tried, in which settings, and with what results. One of the things we tried to do was bring that knowledge together in one place, so that it would outlive any single team. I led the work on the **Saksham Aavishkar Compendium**, which brought together innovations assessed under the project in a form that governments and partners could use.

The lesson I took from it is that collecting knowledge is only half the work. The other half is getting it into the hands of people who will use it, at the moment they need it. A compendium on a website helps. A network of people who know the compendium exists, and can point a colleague to the right page, helps far more.

## YO.DH: young professionals shaping the field

One network I have been glad to be part of is **YO.DH (Youth Ownership of Digital Health)**. PATH describes it as "a volunteer-led community for digital health professionals aged 35 and under." It was launched in July 2023 with support from the Gates Foundation, in partnership with International Innovation Corps, Digital Square and WomenLift Health, and has grown to over 1,000 members in 44 countries. Its work rests on three pillars: community engagement, capacity-building and networking.

What I value about YO.DH is that it treats young professionals as contributors to the field, not only as learners. People early in their careers are often the ones closest to the work: building the forms, cleaning the data, sitting with frontline workers as they test a new app. They see problems and solutions that do not always reach senior meetings. YO.DH gives them a place to share that experience across countries and to learn from peers facing similar problems in very different health systems.

In September 2024, the YO.DH Digest chose USAID Saksham as its project in focus, and interviewed Saksham's Chief of Party, Vaibhao Ambhore, and me about how the project was using digital health to improve maternal and newborn care. When asked about the challenges, I said that health staff "already have their hands full", and that throwing another digital tool at them without thinking it through can feel overwhelming, so the goal of our dashboard was "to reduce their workload, not add to it." It was a small moment, but it captured what I like about the community: it gives practitioners a place to share what they are learning, and to have it heard by people working on the same problems elsewhere. The people who will be running health information systems in 2040 are early in their careers now. Networks like YO.DH are where many of them are learning to work together across borders.

## What makes the exchange work

From the networks I have taken part in, a few things seem to separate the ones that matter from the ones that only meet:

- **Someone does the work between meetings.** Networks run on follow-up: tracking who asked what, who offered help and what happened next.
- **The knowledge is concrete.** A tested template, a contact who has done it before or a list of what went wrong is worth more than a general presentation.
- **Government stakeholders are in the room.** Knowledge only changes a health system when it reaches the people who can act on it.
- **Everyone contributes, not only the largest organisations.** Some of the most useful lessons come from small teams and individuals.

Digital health will keep growing faster than any one organisation can keep up with. The tools to deploy it are increasingly open and free. What remains scarce is shared knowledge, and the trust to pass it on. Networks are how we build both.
