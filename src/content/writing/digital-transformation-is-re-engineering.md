In 1990, Michael Hammer wrote an article in the *Harvard Business Review* with a blunt title: "Reengineering Work: Don't Automate, Obliterate". His argument was that companies were spending fortunes putting computers on top of old processes and getting old results faster. Real gains came only when they rethought the work itself.

Public health keeps relearning a version of that lesson. We talk about digital transformation, and we often mean deploying technology. But a system records the work that is done. If the work does not change, the system faithfully records the old reality. This is the story of one indicator, in a few districts, that taught me this more clearly than anything else.

## A portal for every pregnancy

India took an ambitious step more than a decade ago: tracking pregnant women and children by name, rather than only counting them. The Ministry of Health and Family Welfare describes its Mother and Child Tracking System as built on name-based tracking of each beneficiary, and the RCH (Reproductive and Child Health) portal that succeeded it as designed for early identification and tracking of individuals across the reproductive life cycle. In principle, every pregnant woman could be listed, followed through her check-ups and linked to care.

In the districts where I worked in Chhattisgarh under USAID's Saksham project, including Kanker, the first part of that promise had largely been met. **Most pregnant women were being listed.** That is worth saying plainly, because it is easy to overlook. Name-based registration at that scale depends on thousands of frontline workers doing careful, repetitive work every day, and they were doing it.

## The next problem

Solving one problem has a way of revealing the next one.

Once most pregnancies were on the list, a different number stood out. Only **2%** of pregnancies were being identified as high risk. That was hard to believe. These were districts with difficult maternal health outcomes, and a long-standing planning assumption in WHO and UN guidance on emergency obstetric care is that around **15%** of pregnant women will develop complications. A figure of 2% did not mean that the women were safer than expected. It meant that the risk was not being seen.

The portal had a place to record a high-risk pregnancy. The question was why so little was being recorded there.

## What was really needed

When we looked at the work rather than the system, the answer was not technical. To identify a high-risk pregnancy, an ASHA or ANM has to know what to look for: the danger signs, the history that matters, the readings that should raise concern. Many frontline workers knew some of these but not a consistent set, and nothing in their routine prompted them to go through it with every woman.

So the indicator could only move if the people at the front line were supported to recognise risk. Saksham worked with core groups of frontline workers, the ASHAs, ANMs and other health workers who knew their communities best, to identify high-risk pregnancies and plan follow-up for each woman.

## The fix

Three things made the difference.

**Sensitisation.** ASHAs and ANMs were trained and supported on how a high-risk pregnancy is identified: which signs, which history and which measurements matter, and why.

**A simple tool.** A clear list of high-risk signs gave every worker the same reference, so identification no longer depended on memory or on who happened to be doing the visit.

**A paper register.** This was the decision I learned most from. The obvious instinct, for someone working in digital health, would have been to build an app. But the frontline workers' comfort of choice was a paper register. It was familiar, it worked without a phone or a network, and it fitted the way they already worked. So the high-risk information was recorded first in a register, and the register data was then uploaded, so that district and state teams could track high-risk pregnancies and follow them up.

Over time, the share of pregnancies identified as high risk rose from **2% to 15%**, in line with what would be expected.

## The point

The digital system was not redesigned at all. No new feature was added to the portal and no new app was deployed. What changed was the work around the system: what frontline workers knew, the tool they used and the way information travelled from a woman's home to a state review.

That is what I mean by re-engineering the work. Technology is a powerful part of transformation, but it is only one part. The other part is understanding how the work is actually done, by the people who do it, and changing that with them. Sometimes that means building something new. Sometimes, as here, it means choosing a paper register because that is what the people doing the work trust.

It is also continuous. Once most women were listed, we could see that high-risk pregnancies were missing. Once high-risk pregnancies were being found, the next questions came into view: were those women being followed up, referred in time, delivering in the right facility? Every problem solved reveals the next one. A digital system does not end that cycle. At its best, it makes the next problem visible sooner.
