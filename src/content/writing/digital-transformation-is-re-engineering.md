In 1990, Michael Hammer wrote an article in the Harvard Business Review with a blunt title: *Reengineering Work: Don't Automate, Obliterate.* His argument was that companies were spending fortunes putting computers on top of old processes, and getting old results faster. The real gains came only when they asked why the process existed at all, and redesigned it.

Thirty-five years later, public health is relearning the same lesson. We call it digital transformation, and too often we mean deploying technology. The technology is perhaps half of it. The other half is changing how the work is done, and without that half, the first half rarely works.

## A portal for every pregnancy

India took a bold step more than a decade ago: tracking every pregnant woman and child by name, rather than counting them in aggregate. The Mother and Child Tracking System, and later the RCH (Reproductive and Child Health) portal, were built so that each woman's pregnancy could be followed from registration through antenatal check-ups to delivery and beyond. Line lists of women could be generated by facility and by health worker, and in principle every high-risk pregnancy could be identified and followed up.

That was a genuine digital transformation of the data. On paper, it should have solved the problem of high-risk pregnancies slipping through the system.

It did not, at least not on its own. A commonly used estimate is that around a fifth or more of pregnancies in India carry some high-risk factor: severe anaemia, high blood pressure, previous caesarean, too young or too old, short spacing between births, and others. Yet in the districts where I worked in Chhattisgarh, only about **2%** of pregnancies were being identified as high-risk. The portal had a field for it. The field was mostly empty.

## Why the portal did not fix it

When we looked at the process rather than the system, the reasons were obvious:

- **Identification depended on tests that were not being done.** You cannot record severe anaemia if haemoglobin is not measured, or hypertension if blood pressure is not checked properly at the visit.
- **No one had a clear checklist.** Frontline workers knew some risk factors but not a consistent list, and nothing in their routine prompted them to go through it.
- **Data entry happened far from the woman.** Records were often entered later, by someone else, from paper registers. A risk factor not written on paper never reached the portal.
- **Flagging led nowhere.** Even when a woman was flagged, there was no defined next step: who follows up, by when, and where she should deliver.
- **Incentives rewarded counts.** Workers were measured on registrations and visits, not on whether high-risk women were found and managed.

None of these is a technology problem. Each is a **process** problem: who does what, when, with what tool, and what happens next. A portal sitting on top of that process records its gaps faithfully.

## What changed the number

Working with frontline workers, mostly women, through core groups that defined the problems and designed the solutions, we did the unglamorous part:

1. **A simple high-risk pregnancy checklist** for frontline workers to go through with every woman, so that identification no longer depended on memory.
2. **Line-list analysis** of the data already in the portal, to find women whose records showed risk factors but who had not been flagged, and to target follow-up.
3. **Clear follow-up roles:** who visits a flagged woman, when, and which facility she should be linked to for delivery.

In our intervention areas, the share of pregnancies identified as high-risk rose from **2% to 15%**. The portal did not change. The work around it did.

## The 50% rule

I have come to think of digital transformation as having two halves, and of the technology as at most half the game:

- **The digital half:** the system, the data model, the devices, the connectivity, the training on how to use the app.
- **The re-engineering half:** redesigning the process the system supports, clarifying roles and handoffs, changing what people are measured on, and making sure the output of the system triggers action.

Most programmes budget, plan and report on the first half. The second half is assumed to follow. It rarely does. If no one adopts the new way of working, the transformation is not real. It is a new system recording the old reality.

## Questions to ask before going digital

Before building or buying any digital health system, it is worth asking:

1. **What process is this system supporting, step by step, today?** Draw it, with the real people and real delays, not the one in the guidelines.
2. **Which steps should not exist at all?** Hammer's "obliterate". Double entry, duplicate registers and reports nobody reads are candidates.
3. **What will each person do differently on the day the system goes live?** If the answer is "the same thing, but also enter it in the app", expect resistance and poor data.
4. **What action does each output trigger, and who owns it?** A flag without an owner is decoration.
5. **What will we measure to know the work has changed, not just the system?** Use of the output, not number of logins.

The best digital health systems I have seen were designed by people who first understood the work deeply, and who were willing to change it. Technology made the new way of working possible. It did not, by itself, make it happen.
