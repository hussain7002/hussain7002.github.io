When you work on innovation in public health, innovators come to you. Under USAID's Saksham project, through the wider Aavishkar initiative, many of them approached us: start-ups, researchers and established companies with devices and digital tools for maternal, newborn and child health. Part of our job was to look for innovations that could help, and part of it was to give honest guidance on how they might fit into the public health system. Some of the best of them were brought together in the Saksham Aavishkar Compendium, which I led.

What struck me again and again was how often a genuinely good product ran into a very ordinary problem. Not a flaw in the science, but a mismatch between the conditions it was designed for and the conditions it would actually meet.

## A jacket for newborn jaundice

One example has stayed with me.

Newborn jaundice is common, and severe cases need phototherapy: exposing the baby's skin to a particular kind of light. In many facilities this means a bulky unit, a free cot and a power supply that does not fail. So we were excited to see a wearable phototherapy device, designed in Europe, that wrapped the baby in a soft, light-emitting jacket. It was elegant, it let mothers hold their babies during treatment, and it could in principle bring phototherapy closer to home.

Then we read the specifications. The device was rated to operate at ambient temperatures of up to about 30°C. Many of the districts where it would be most useful reach 45°C in summer, and many facilities have no air conditioning. To use it safely, a facility would need air conditioning, a stable power supply to run it, and a whole set-up around the device that most sub-centres and primary health centres do not have.

It was a great product. One practical detail stood between it and the women and babies it could have helped.

## The factors that decide

That example is not unusual. Most health technologies are designed and tested in conditions very different from those of a primary health centre in a hot, humid, crowded district. Over time I came to think of the practical factors in a few groups.

### The environment

| Factor | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Temperature** | Devices, batteries, reagents and test strips all have operating and storage ranges. Many districts in South Asia exceed them for months each year. |
| **Humidity and monsoon** | Moisture damages electronics, degrades consumables and affects some sensor readings. |
| **Dust** | Fine dust gets into fans, optics and ports, especially in dry seasons and near construction. |
| **Geography and transport** | A device that must be carried between villages, over rough roads or by boat, needs to survive being carried. Floods cut off supply routes. |

### Infrastructure

| Factor | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Power** | Outages, voltage swings and the lack of backup are common in peripheral facilities. A device that needs uninterrupted mains power may spend much of its life switched off. |
| **Internet** | Connectivity in rural facilities can be patchy. Tools that depend on the cloud need to work offline and sync later. |
| **Phones** | Frontline workers often use low-cost Android phones with limited memory, older operating system versions and shared use within families. An app built for the latest phone may not run, or may crowd out everything else. |

### People and work

| Factor | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Language and literacy** | Interfaces, instructions and alerts must work in local languages and for users with varied literacy. |
| **Caseload and time** | A device that takes ten minutes per patient is a different proposition in a clinic that sees a hundred patients before lunch. |
| **Workflow and training** | Who will use it, at which visit, and what happens to the result? A tool that adds a step without removing one tends to be skipped. |
| **Cultural acceptability** | Some devices touch the body in ways, or at moments, that patients or families may not accept. |

### The long tail

| Factor | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Consumables and spare parts** | Strips, probes, cartridges and batteries have to be available, affordable and procurable year after year. |
| **Service and maintenance** | Who repairs it, and how far away are they? Biomedical engineering support is thin outside cities. |
| **Cleaning and infection control** | Can it be cleaned with what the facility has, between every patient? |
| **Disposal** | Batteries, electronics and single-use components eventually need safe disposal. |

### The system

| Factor | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Affordability and procurement** | Public procurement has its own rules, cycles and price expectations. A product priced for private hospitals may never fit a government tender. |
| **Regulatory approval** | Medical devices, including software with a medical purpose, may need approval from the national regulator for their intended use. |
| **Data integration** | If a device produces data, can it reach the government's own systems, or does it create one more silo? |

## Why this matters for AI too

None of this disappears when the technology is artificial intelligence. An AI model still runs on a device, in a facility, in someone's hands. It still needs power, a phone that can run it and a network to update it. It still has to fit into a crowded clinic and produce something a health worker can act on. AI adds its own questions about evidence and validation, which I have written about separately. But a perfectly validated model on a phone that overheats in July is still a model that does not get used.

## A different kind of question

The lesson I took from Saksham is that the most useful question to ask about a health technology is often not "how good is it?" but "**will it work here?**" That question is not hostile to innovation. It is the question that turns a promising product into one that reaches people. The best innovators we met welcomed it, because they wanted their products to work in the places that needed them most.

And the answer is rarely a simple yes or no. Sometimes a product needs a small redesign: a wider temperature range, an offline mode, a cheaper consumable. Sometimes it fits one level of care but not another. Sometimes the right answer is to wait. Asking early, before a pilot or a purchase, saves everyone time, and gives good ideas their best chance of lasting.

These factors are not unique to India. Much of South and South-East Asia and sub-Saharan Africa shares the same heat, humidity, power cuts and crowded clinics. A product that can work in those conditions can work for a very large share of the world's mothers and children.
