The last few years have been a hard lesson in dependence. During the pandemic, countries discovered how quickly vaccines, oxygen, protective equipment and even basic medicines could become scarce when supply chains were disrupted or when other countries needed them first. Since then, tensions over trade, technology and security have made many governments look again at what they rely on others for.

The lesson most of them have drawn is about **resilience**: the ability to keep critical systems running when the world around them is unpredictable. That does not mean doing everything alone. It means knowing which things are critical, and making sure they stay in hands you trust.

Artificial intelligence is now on that list. As AI becomes part of how governments deliver services, how hospitals work and how health systems make decisions, the question of who builds it, who runs it and on whose terms is no longer only a technical one. That is what people mean by **sovereign AI**.

## What sovereign AI means

There is no single definition, but the idea is straightforward. A country has sovereign AI when it has enough control over the key ingredients of AI, its data, computing capacity, models, skills and rules, to use the technology on its own terms. It can keep sensitive data within its borders. It can build or adapt models that understand its languages and its people. And its critical systems will not stop working if a foreign provider changes its prices, its policies or its priorities.

Sovereignty is not the same as isolation. The World Bank's *World Development Report 2026* argues that most developing countries should begin by **adopting** and **adapting** existing AI tools, rather than trying to build the most advanced models themselves, and presents its framework as a way to benefit from AI without becoming dependent on a single supplier. Open models, published by companies and researchers around the world, make that kind of adaptation possible. In practice, sovereignty is often less about building everything at home and more about having real choices.

In health, the stakes are particularly clear. Health data is among the most sensitive data a government holds. Models trained mostly on data from high-income countries may not reflect local diseases, populations or ways of delivering care. And a health service that depends on an AI system it cannot inspect, adapt or keep running is vulnerable in exactly the way the pandemic taught us to avoid.

## India's push

India has made AI a visible national priority. In February 2026 it hosted the **India AI Impact Summit** in New Delhi. According to All India Radio's News On AIR, the summit concluded with the adoption of the **New Delhi Declaration on AI Impact**, endorsed by 88 countries and international organisations and organised around seven pillars, or "Chakras". The report also refers to voluntary guiding principles on resilient, innovative and efficient AI. Resilience, in other words, was written into the summit's outcomes.

Alongside the diplomacy, Indian organisations have been building models designed for Indian languages and needs. Sarvam AI, for example, has said that in April 2025 it was selected under the IndiaAI Mission to build an indigenous foundation model from scratch, one "designed for voice, and fluent in Indian languages." For a country where most people do not use English in daily life, and where health services are delivered in dozens of languages, models of this kind matter a great deal.

## SAHI

Health has its own strand of this work. In February 2026, the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare published the **Strategy for Artificial Intelligence in Healthcare for India (SAHI)**, developed by a committee chaired by the CEO of the National Health Authority. The document notes that it was closely supported by the National Health Authority, the WHO South-East Asia Regional Office, the ICMR's National Institute for Research in Digital Health and Data Science, the Koita Foundation and the Gates Foundation.

Its vision is "to enable the safe, ethical, evidence-based, and inclusive use of artificial intelligence across India's healthcare system, leveraging digital public infrastructure and institutional strengths to drive responsible innovation, expand access to high quality affordable care, improve health outcomes, and position India as a global leader in responsible AI for healthcare." SAHI describes AI as "a strategic enabler of health system strengthening", and says its adoption "must be anchored in public interest, trust, and long-term system resilience." It is explicit that it is "not conceived as a rigid or prescriptive document", but as "a guiding and enabling framework", and a "living document" to be refined as technology and evidence evolve.

SAHI is built on seven governing principles, which it calls **sutras**: trust is the foundation; people first; innovation over restraint; fairness and equity; accountability; understandable by design; and safety, resilience and sustainability. The last of these asks for AI that is "safe, scientifically sound and dependable under real world conditions."

Around these principles sit five strategic pillars:

1. **Governance, Regulation, and Trust**
2. **Health Data and Digital Infrastructure**
3. **Workforce, Institutional Capacity, and Change Management**
4. **Research, Innovation, and Evidence Generation**
5. **Ecosystem Enablement and Global Leadership**

Launched alongside SAHI at the India AI Impact Summit was **BODH (Benchmarking Open Data Platform for Health AI)**, developed by IIT Kanpur in collaboration with the National Health Authority. The Government describes BODH as providing "a structured mechanism for testing and validating Health AI solutions before deployment at scale."

## A few comments on SAHI

Reading SAHI with sovereign AI in mind, several things stand out to me.

**Resilience is written into its foundations.** Resilience appears in SAHI's framing of why AI should be adopted, in its seventh sutra and in the outcomes it aims for. That is the same instinct that lies behind sovereign AI: treating AI in health as infrastructure that has to keep working, not just a set of products.

**It speaks directly to self-reliance.** The research and innovation pillar, in SAHI's words, "emphasises self-reliance in indigenous AI solutions", while insisting that innovation remain clinically credible and aligned with public health priorities. That is sovereignty framed in a practical, health-centred way.

**It cares about whose data shapes the models.** SAHI recommends that national health data "should reflect population-scale realities rather than being concentrated in high-resource facilities." For a country as diverse as India, that may be the most important form of sovereignty of all: models that work for patients in district hospitals and rural clinics, not only in metropolitan ones.

**Evidence scales with risk.** SAHI links evaluation requirements to the risk of an AI system. It asks for benchmarking against reference standards, sensitivity and specificity aligned to the intended use, subgroup analysis to detect bias, and an assessment of data shift and model drift before deployment. Paired with BODH, this gives Indian health systems a way to judge AI on its performance for Indian patients.

**It takes institutions seriously.** Sovereignty is not only about data centres and models. SAHI recommends designated AI units within health departments and institutions, responsible for use-case prioritisation, procurement, deployment oversight and lifecycle management, and it calls for public procurement of health AI to be redefined. In a federal system where capacity varies widely between states, this is where much of the real work will lie.

**Being a living, guiding framework cuts both ways.** It allows SAHI to move with the technology, which is essential. It also means its impact will depend on how far states, hospitals, programmes and developers take it up.

## A light comparison: Singapore

India is not alone. Singapore's national AI programme, AI Singapore, develops **SEA-LION**, which it describes as "a family of efficient, open-source, multilingual, multimodal language models" designed "to understand Southeast Asia's diverse languages, cultures, and contexts." It works across eleven or more Southeast Asian languages and is openly available for research and commercial use.

The approaches differ in scale and focus, but the underlying idea is similar: a country, or a region, that wants AI to serve its people well needs models that understand its languages and contexts, and some ability to shape them itself. Notably, both put weight on openness. Sovereignty, in these examples, is pursued through open models and shared tools rather than closed ones.

## Why it matters

My own view is simple. For health, local resilience is paramount. The pandemic showed what happens when critical supplies depend entirely on decisions made elsewhere. AI is becoming part of the critical infrastructure of health systems, so the same logic applies: countries need enough capacity, data, models and people to use AI on their own terms, and to keep it working when they need it most.

That does not mean building everything at home, and it does not mean closing the door to the best tools from anywhere in the world. It means having real choices, and the institutions to make them well. SAHI, with BODH alongside it, is a meaningful step in that direction for India's health system. Its real test, like that of any strategy, will be how far it travels from the national level to the hospitals, programmes and frontline services where AI will actually be used.
