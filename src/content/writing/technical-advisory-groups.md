Global health runs on expert advice more than most people realise. WHO has technical advisory groups for almost everything. SAGE, its Strategic Advisory Group of Experts on immunization, shapes vaccine policy for the world. STAGE does the same for maternal, newborn, child and adolescent health and nutrition. Countries have their own versions: India's National Technical Advisory Group on Immunization (NTAGI) has advised the government on every major vaccine decision for more than two decades, including during COVID-19.

The idea is simple. Governments make decisions under pressure, with incomplete evidence. A standing group of the country's best experts, with a clear mandate and a route into policy, can review that evidence calmly and say what it means. Done well, an advisory group makes policy better and more credible. Done badly, it becomes a committee that meets, minutes, and is ignored.

Between 2023 and 2024, at PATH, I supported the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare in establishing a **National Technical Advisory Group for maternal, newborn, child and adolescent health and nutrition (MNCAH+N)** at the All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS), New Delhi, under the USAID-funded Saksham programme. Its role is to review evidence, innovations and best practices in MNCH and make recommendations to the Ministry. It has since delivered policy briefs that inform national programme decisions.

This is what I learned about making such a group work.

## 1. Start with the decisions, not the experts

The temptation is to begin by inviting the most famous names. The better starting point is: **what decisions does the Ministry need help with in the next two years?** Which guidelines are due for revision, which innovations are waiting for a decision, which programme questions keep coming back? The answers define the mandate, and the mandate defines who should be in the room.

An advisory group without a decision to inform will find something to discuss. It will not necessarily be what the government needs.

## 2. Write the terms of reference as if they will be tested

Clear terms of reference are what turn a gathering into an institution:
- **Mandate:** what the group advises on, and what it does not.
- **Membership:** disciplines needed, how members are chosen, terms, and how they rotate.
- **Conflicts of interest:** declared in writing, reviewed, and managed, especially when the group reviews products or innovations.
- **Outputs:** what form advice takes (policy brief, recommendation, review) and to whom it goes.
- **Cadence:** how often it meets, and how urgent questions are handled between meetings.

## 3. Housing matters

Where a group sits shapes how it is seen. Housing this one at AIIMS, India's premier medical institution, gave it scientific credibility and a neutral home, separate from any one programme or partner. It also gave it access to the clinical departments whose expertise it needed.

## 4. Get the right people, and the right mix

The best groups combine clinical experts (obstetricians, paediatricians, neonatologists), public health and programme people who understand delivery at scale, researchers who can read evidence critically, and representatives of those who will implement the advice. A group of only senior clinicians will give excellent clinical advice that may not survive contact with a district hospital.

Bringing very senior experts on board takes patience: personal outreach, a clear explanation of why their time will matter, and respect for their institutions' processes. Formal setup inside a large institution and a ministry involves many steps: approvals, letters, nominations. That work is invisible and essential.

## 5. A secretariat is the engine

Experts give their time; they cannot also run the machinery. Behind every effective advisory group is a small secretariat that:
- prepares evidence summaries and background papers before each meeting
- frames the specific questions the group must answer
- records decisions precisely and circulates them quickly
- follows up on what happened to each recommendation.

That last step is the one most often skipped, and the one that matters most. If members never hear what became of their advice, they stop giving it seriously.

## 6. Design the route into policy

Advice has to land somewhere. The group's recommendations should go to a named person or body in the Ministry, in a format they can use, at a time that matches the decision cycle. A twenty-page review arriving after a guideline has been finalised is wasted. A two-page brief arriving before it can change it.

## 7. Measure influence, not meetings

The useful measures of an advisory group are not how often it met. They are: how many recommendations were adopted, how quickly advice reached decision-makers, and whether the evidence base for policy improved.

## Why this matters for digital health and AI

Many countries in South-East Asia are now creating advisory structures for digital health and AI, often for the first time. The same principles apply, with two additions. First, the field moves fast, so advisory groups need ways to respond in weeks, not years. Second, the line between expert and vendor is often blurred in technology, which makes conflict-of-interest rules even more important.

An advisory group is one of the cheapest and most powerful tools a ministry has. It costs little more than the time of people who care about the problem. What it needs is design: a clear mandate, the right mix, a strong secretariat, and a route into decisions.
