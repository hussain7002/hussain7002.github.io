Much of global health runs on a mechanism most people never hear about: the technical advisory group.

WHO has many of them. Each brings together a small number of eminent people, clinicians, researchers, programme leaders and public health specialists, from different countries, cultures and health systems. They meet regularly, review the evidence on a set of questions and advise WHO on what it should prioritise and recommend. Their members serve in their individual capacity, not as representatives of their governments or institutions.

I find this a fascinating idea. A guideline written by one institution in one country will always carry that country's assumptions. A group that brings together, say, a paediatrician from India, an epidemiologist from Brazil, a nutritionist from Kenya and a health systems researcher from Europe will argue its way to something more robust. Each member brings real implementation experience, the kind that only comes from having tried to make policy work in a particular place. The result is advice that has already been tested against many realities before it reaches anyone.

## STAGE

For maternal, newborn, child and adolescent health and nutrition, WHO's group is **STAGE**, the Strategic and Technical Advisory Group of Experts. WHO describes it as "the principal advisory group" to the organisation in this area, established by the Director-General in 2019 to "provide strategic and technical advice to WHO" and to "advise WHO on global priorities and emerging issues." Its work feeds into the guidance that countries use to shape their own programmes for mothers, newborns, children and adolescents.

One of its members is **Dr Narendra Kumar Arora**. According to his WHO biography, he is a member of STAGE and co-chairs its working group on anaemia, and he has served on WHO's Global Advisory Committee on Vaccine Safety. In India, he has long been associated with the National Technical Advisory Group on Immunization (NTAGI), and he chaired its COVID-19 working group during the pandemic. He was a professor at AIIMS New Delhi from 1983 to 2007 and has led the INCLEN Trust International. Few people have as much experience of how expert advice can shape national health policy.

## Bringing the idea home

NTAGI showed what a national advisory group could do for immunisation in India. Under USAID's Saksham project, we asked whether the same model could work for maternal, newborn, child and adolescent health and nutrition, an area with many programmes, many innovations and many decisions waiting to be made.

The idea was a **National Technical Advisory Group (NTAG) for MNCAH+N**, based at AIIMS New Delhi, with the head of the Department of Obstetrics and Gynaecology on board. Dr Arora agreed to chair it, and he was its driving force. He brought together people who do not often sit at the same table: researchers and academics, non-profit organisations working in the field, and people from the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare. The aim was to review evidence, innovations and best practices, and to offer considered advice on what deserved to be taken forward.

My own role was a supporting one. I was part of a small, three-person programme management unit under Saksham that acted as the group's secretariat until a formal one could be set up. That meant the unglamorous work that every advisory group depends on: preparing background papers, framing the questions for each meeting, recording what was discussed and decided, and following up afterwards. It gave me a close view of how much happens between meetings, and how much a group of very senior people depends on someone keeping the threads together.

## What happened

The group met and worked for about a year and a half. It produced a few policy papers, which were shared within the group.

It was never formally instituted. Formal recognition of a national advisory body takes time, approvals and a clear institutional home, and that process was still under way when the funding for the work was cut. Without a secretariat and without formal status, the group could not continue.

I do not tell this as a story of failure. For a year and a half, some of India's most experienced people in maternal and child health met regularly to think together about what the country's programmes needed, and they produced work of real value. But it is a candid example of a pattern I have written about elsewhere: a good initiative that depends on project funding, and has not yet become part of the system's own structures, is fragile however good it is.

## What I took from it

Watching the group at close quarters taught me three things about what advisory groups need.

**A formal home and mandate.** WHO's groups work partly because they are established by the Director-General, with a defined role and a clear route for their advice. A national group needs the same: formal recognition, terms of reference and a named place in the policy process. Goodwill and eminent members are not enough on their own.

**A secretariat that outlasts any one project.** Experts give their time; they cannot also run the machinery. The secretariat is what turns meetings into advice and advice into follow-up. If it depends on one project's budget, the whole group does too.

**A route from advice to decision.** The value of an advisory group lies in what happens to its recommendations. The groups that last are the ones whose advice visibly reaches the people who decide.

None of this diminishes the idea. I still think technical advisory groups are one of the best mechanisms global health has invented: a way of putting the experience of many countries and many careers to work on shared problems. Seeing one up close, and seeing one end too early, made me appreciate both how valuable they are and how deliberately they have to be built.
